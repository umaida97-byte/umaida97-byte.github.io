The Burslem rental market

The housing is mostly dense Victorian terraces built around the old potbanks, especially in Middleport, Longport and the streets around the town centre. Cobridge has some larger Victorian and Edwardian houses, and Sneyd Green and Smallthorne have more interwar and post-war semi-detached homes.

Demand comes mainly from local tenants: couples and families looking for an affordable two- or three-bedroom home within reach of the town centre and the bus routes into Hanley. Match days at Vale Park, Port Vale's ground, are a reminder of how local and close-knit the area is. Many tenants have lived in Burslem all their lives and want to stay.

Advice for Burslem landlords

  • Yield over growth. Low purchase prices mean Burslem often produces some of the strongest gross yields in the city. Capital growth has historically been slower, so it suits landlords investing for income.
  • Budget for older buildings. Rewiring, damp treatment and roof repairs come up more often in pre-1900 terraces. We flag issues early at our inspections, before they become expensive.
  • Watch the regeneration. Restoration of heritage buildings such as the Wedgwood Institute and the reuse of old pottery sites are slowly changing parts of the town centre. It is worth considering when deciding which streets to buy in.

Living in Burslem

Burslem has a strong local identity: the gilded angel on the Old Town Hall, Burslem Park (a restored Victorian park), the Burslem School of Art, and Middleport Pottery on the Trent & Mersey Canal, a working Victorian pottery that is open to visitors. Buses run frequently to Hanley and Tunstall, Longport railway station is on the Crewe to Derby line, and the A500 is a few minutes away.
